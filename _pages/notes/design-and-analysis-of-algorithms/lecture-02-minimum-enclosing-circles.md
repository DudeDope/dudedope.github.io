## Learning objectives

- Formulate the minimum enclosing circle problem as a minimax optimization problem.
- Prove that an optimal circle is supported by at most three input points.
- Construct candidate circles from pairs and non-collinear triples.
- Prove correctness and the $\Theta(n^4)$ running time of the naive algorithm.
- Solve representative geometric examples completely.

## 1. Problem statement and minimax formulation

Let

$$
S=\lbrace{}P_1,P_2,\dots,P_n\rbrace\subset \mathbb{R}^2
$$

be a finite set of points. For a candidate center $P\in\mathbb{R}^2$, define its worst-case distance to the data by

$$
F(P)=\max_{1\le i\le n}\|P-P_i\|_2.
$$

> **Definition 2.1 --- Minimum enclosing circle.**  
> A _minimum enclosing circle_ (MEC) of $S$ is a closed disk
>
> $$
> \overline B(P^\star,R^\star)=\lbrace{}Q\in\mathbb{R}^2:\|Q-P^\star\|_2\le R^\star\rbrace
> $$
>
> with minimum possible radius among all disks containing $S$.

The optimization problem is

$$
P^\star\in \arg\min_{P\in\mathbb{R}^2} F(P),
\qquad
R^\star=F(P^\star).
$$

> **Proposition 2.2 --- Equivalence of the minimax point and the MEC center.**  
> A point $P^\star$ minimizes $F(P)=\max_i\lVert{}P-P_i\rVert_2$ if and only if the disk centered at $P^\star$ with radius $F(P^\star)$ is a minimum enclosing circle.

**Proof.**

For any $P$, the disk $\overline B(P,F(P))$ contains every $P_i$ by the definition of the maximum. Hence every candidate center produces an enclosing disk whose radius is exactly its objective value.

If $P^\star$ minimizes $F$, then an enclosing disk of radius $R<R^\star=F(P^\star)$ would have some center $Q$ and would imply $F(Q)\le R<R^\star$, contradicting optimality. Conversely, if $\overline B(P^\star,R^\star)$ is an MEC, then $F(P^\star)\le R^\star$. Equality must hold after shrinking any unused radius, and no other $Q$ can satisfy $F(Q)<R^\star$ because that would yield a smaller enclosing disk.

$\square$

> **Additional context.**  
> This explanation was added to make the lecture self-contained.
>
> The function $F$ is continuous because it is the maximum of finitely many continuous functions. It is also coercive: $F(P)\to\infty$ as $\lVert{}P\rVert\to\infty$. Therefore a minimizer exists. In Euclidean space, the MEC center is unique. One proof uses the parallelogram identity: if two distinct centers $A,B$ had the same optimal radius $R$, then their midpoint $M$ would satisfy
>
> $$
> \|P_i-M\|^2=\frac{\|P_i-A\|^2+\|P_i-B\|^2}{2}-\frac{\|A-B\|^2}{4}
> < R^2
> $$
>
> for every $i$, producing a strictly smaller enclosing radius, a contradiction.

## 2. Why two or three points determine the optimum

A point $P_i$ is a _support point_ if it lies on the boundary of the MEC, that is, $\lVert{}P_i-P^\star\rVert=R^\star$.

> **Theorem 2.3 --- Support-set characterization.**  
> For a nonempty finite set in $\mathbb{R}^2$, its MEC is determined by one of the following:
>
> 1.  one point, when all input points coincide;
> 2.  two support points that are endpoints of a diameter;
> 3.  three non-collinear support points whose circumcircle is the MEC and whose triangle contains the center in its convex hull.
>
> Consequently, for distinct non-coincident points, it suffices to test circles generated by all pairs and all non-collinear triples.

**Proof.**

At least one point lies on the boundary; otherwise the radius could be reduced slightly. Suppose first that exactly one distinct point $A$ is on the boundary. Move the center a sufficiently small distance toward $A$. The distance to $A$ decreases, while all other points had positive slack and remain inside for a small move. The radius can then be reduced, contradicting minimality.

Suppose exactly two boundary points $A,B$ support the disk. If the center $O$ is not the midpoint of $AB$, then $AB$ is a proper chord, not a diameter. Moving $O$ slightly toward the chord along its perpendicular bisector reduces both $\lVert{}O-A\rVert$ and $\lVert{}O-B\rVert$; all other points remain inside by slack. Again the radius could be reduced. Hence $O$ is the midpoint and $AB$ is a diameter.

Otherwise at least three boundary points are needed. Choose a minimal support subset. In the plane, three non-collinear points determine a unique circle. The center must lie in the convex hull of the support points: if all support points lie in some open half-plane through the center, moving the center slightly toward them reduces all active distances and preserves all slack constraints. Thus three support points suffice and their circumcircle is the MEC.

$\square$

> **Editorial note.**  
> The lecture wording says that for three boundary points “the center is contained within the acute triangle.” The precise statement is: the circumcenter lies strictly inside the triangle exactly when the triangle is acute. For a right triangle, the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse, so the MEC is already determined by the two hypotenuse endpoints. For an obtuse triangle, the circumcircle is not minimal; the longest side is a diameter of the MEC.

## 3. Constructing candidate circles

### 3.1. Candidate from two points

For $A,B\in\mathbb{R}^2$, the circle having $AB$ as a diameter has

$$
O=\frac{A+B}{2},
\qquad
R=\frac{\|A-B\|}{2}.
$$

### 3.2. Candidate from three non-collinear points

Let $A=(x_1,y_1)$, $B=(x_2,y_2)$, and $C=(x_3,y_3)$. The circumcenter $O=(u,v)$ satisfies

$$
\|O-A\|^2=\|O-B\|^2=\|O-C\|^2.
$$

Subtracting the first equation from the other two eliminates the quadratic terms and gives the linear system

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
2(x_2-x_1) & 2(y_2-y_1)\\
2(x_3-x_1) & 2(y_3-y_1)
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_2^2+y_2^2-x_1^2-y_1^2\\
x_3^2+y_3^2-x_1^2-y_1^2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The determinant is nonzero exactly when $A,B,C$ are non-collinear. After solving, set $R=\lVert{}O-A\rVert$.

## 4. Naive exhaustive algorithm

**Algorithm --- Naive minimum enclosing circle.**

```text
Input: Points P[1], ..., P[n] in R^2
Output: A minimum-radius circle containing all points

bestRadius <- +infinity
bestCircle <- None

for every pair 1 <= i < j <= n:
    C <- circle with P[i]P[j] as a diameter
    if C contains every input point and radius(C) < bestRadius:
        bestCircle <- C
        bestRadius <- radius(C)

for every triple 1 <= i < j < k <= n:
    if P[i], P[j], P[k] are non-collinear:
        C <- their circumcircle
        if C contains every input point and radius(C) < bestRadius:
            bestCircle <- C
            bestRadius <- radius(C)

return bestCircle
```

> **Theorem 2.4 --- Correctness.**  
> The naive algorithm returns the MEC.

**Proof.**

By the support-set characterization, the true MEC is either a diameter circle generated by a pair or a circumcircle generated by a non-collinear triple. Therefore it appears among the candidates enumerated by the algorithm. The containment test removes invalid candidates. Among all valid candidates, choosing one with minimum radius returns the true MEC.

$\square$

> **Proposition 2.5 --- Running time.**  
> The naive algorithm runs in $\Theta(n^4)$ time in the RAM model.

**Proof.**

There are $\binom n2=\Theta(n^2)$ pair candidates and $\binom n3=\Theta(n^3)$ triple candidates. Constructing a candidate takes constant time, while testing whether it contains all points takes $\Theta(n)$ time. Thus

$$
\Theta(n)\left(\binom n2+\binom n3\right)=\Theta(n^4).
$$

$\square$

## 5. Worked examples

### Worked Example 2.6 --- A rectangle

**Problem.**

Find the minimum enclosing circle of the four vertices $S=\lbrace(0,0),(4,0),(4,2),(0,2)\rbrace$.

**Solution.**

Let

$$
S=\lbrace(0,0),(4,0),(4,2),(0,2)\rbrace.
$$

The farthest pair consists of opposite corners, for example $A=(0,0)$ and $C=(4,2)$. Their midpoint is

$$
O=\left(2,1\right),
$$

and the radius of the diameter circle is

$$
R=\frac{\sqrt{(4-0)^2+(2-0)^2}}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{20}}2=\sqrt5.
$$

The other two corners satisfy

$$
\|(4,0)-(2,1)\|=\sqrt5,
\qquad
\|(0,2)-(2,1)\|=\sqrt5.
$$

Hence all four points lie on this circle. No smaller circle can contain the diametrically opposite points $A,C$, because any enclosing circle has radius at least $\lVert{}A-C\rVert/2=\sqrt5$. Therefore this is the MEC.

**Final result.**

$$
O=(2,1),\qquad R=\sqrt{5}.
$$

**Interpretation.**

The opposite corners form a diameter, so the radius lower bound from their distance is attained.

### Worked Example 2.7 --- An acute triangle requiring three support points

**Problem.**

Find the minimum enclosing circle of the acute triangle with vertices $A=(0,0)$, $B=(4,0)$, and $C=(2,3)$.

**Solution.**

Take $A=(0,0)$, $B=(4,0)$, and $C=(2,3)$. By symmetry the circumcenter has the form $O=(2,v)$. Equating distances to $A$ and $C$ gives

$$
(2-0)^2+v^2=(2-2)^2+(v-3)^2.
$$

Thus

$$
4+v^2=v^2-6v+9
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v=\frac56.
$$

Therefore

$$
O=\left(2,\frac56\right),
\qquad
R^2=4+\frac{25}{36}=\frac{169}{36},
\qquad
R=\frac{13}{6}.
$$

The triangle is acute, so no side-diameter circle contains the third vertex. The circumcircle is therefore the MEC and all three points are essential supports.

**Final result.**

$$
O=\left(2,\frac{5}{6}\right),\qquad R=\frac{13}{6}.
$$

**Interpretation.**

All three vertices are essential support points because the triangle is acute.

> **Additional context.**  
> This explanation was added to make the lecture self-contained.
>
> The exhaustive $\Theta(n^4)$ algorithm is useful for proving structure, not for large inputs. Welzl’s randomized incremental algorithm solves the MEC problem in expected $\Theta(n)$ time. Its key idea is to process points in random order while maintaining a small boundary set of at most three points. A full analysis requires backward analysis and is beyond the supplied lectures.

## Questions answered in this lecture

> **Question.**  
> Why must an MEC have at least two essential support points unless all points coincide?

**Answer.**

With only one distinct boundary point, the center can be moved slightly toward it while all other points remain inside by their positive slack; the radius can then be reduced, contradicting minimality.

> **Question.**  
> Why must two support points be diametrically opposite?

**Answer.**

If their chord is not a diameter, moving the center along the perpendicular bisector toward the chord decreases both active distances while preserving all slack constraints, again contradicting minimality.

> **Question.**  
> When do three points, rather than a pair, determine the MEC?

**Answer.**

Three non-collinear support points determine the MEC when their circumcenter lies in their convex hull; for an acute triangle this is the circumcircle, whereas right or obtuse triangles reduce to a longest-side diameter case.

## Lecture summary

The MEC is the disk minimizing the maximum distance from its center to the input points. Its boundary is determined by either a diameter pair or three non-collinear support points, which justifies exhaustive enumeration of pair and triple candidates.
