## From point estimation to interval estimation

A point estimate gives a single value. An interval estimate acknowledges that a different sample would generally produce a different result.

<div class="definition" markdown="1">

**Definition 4.1** (Confidence interval). A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for a fixed parameter $\theta$ is a random interval $[L(S),U(S)]$ satisfying

$$
\mathbb{P}_p\lbrace{}L(S)\le\theta\le U(S)\rbrace\ge 1-\alpha,
$$

where probability is computed over repeated samples from the design.

</div>

The parameter is fixed; the endpoints are random. A 95% confidence procedure means that at least 95% of intervals generated by repeated use of the procedure cover the parameter. After one interval is observed, the frequentist statement is that the procedure has 95% long-run coverage, not that the fixed parameter now has probability 0.95 of lying inside that particular realised interval.

## Canonical exact normal interval

Suppose

$$
X_1,\ldots,X_n\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(\mu,\sigma^2),
$$

with $\sigma$ known. Then

$$
\overline{X}\sim N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right),
\qquad
\frac{\overline{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1).
$$

Let $z_{\alpha/2}$ denote the positive number satisfying

$$
\mathbb{P}(Z>z_{\alpha/2})=\frac\alpha2,
\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
$$

Equivalently, it is the $(1-\alpha/2)$ quantile. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(
-z_{\alpha/2}
\le
\frac{\overline{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
\le
z_{\alpha/2}
\right)=1-\alpha.
$$

Solving the inequalities for $\mu$ yields the exact interval

$$
\boxed{
\overline{X}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}
}.
$$

For 95% confidence, $z_{0.025}\approx1.96$.

If $\sigma$ is unknown and the observations are genuinely iid normal, then

$$
\frac{\overline{X}-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1},
$$

which gives the exact Student interval

$$
\overline{X}\pm t_{n-1,\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
$$

## Confidence intervals for a finite-population mean

Under SRSWOR, the exact sampling distribution of $\overline{y}$ depends on the complete unknown set $Y_1,\ldots,Y_N$. In principle, if all population values were known, one could enumerate all $\binom Nn$ sample means and compute exact design probabilities. But then $\overline{Y}$ would already be known. For an arbitrary unknown finite population, there is no universal exact normal-form interval based only on $\overline{y}$ and $s^2$.

### Finite-population central limit theorem

For a sequence of finite populations, a representative sufficient condition for asymptotic normality is that both sampled and unsampled counts grow and no single unit dominates the population sum of squares. One common form is

$$
n\to\infty,
\qquad
N-n\to\infty,
\qquad
\frac{\max_{1\le j\le N}(Y_j-\overline{Y})^2}
{\sum_{j=1}^{N}(Y_j-\overline{Y})^2}
\to0.
$$

Under suitable regularity conditions of this kind,

$$
\frac{\overline{y}-\overline{Y}}
{\sqrt{(1-f)S_Y^2/n}}
\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1).
$$

This is the finite-population analogue of a central limit theorem, associated with work of Hájek.

Replacing $S_Y$ by $s$ gives the approximate interval

$$
\boxed{
\overline{y}\pm z_{\alpha/2}
\sqrt{1-f}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}
}.
$$

For 95% confidence,

$$
\overline{y}\pm1.96\sqrt{1-f}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
$$

> **Caution.**
>
> For small $n$, replacing $1.96$ by a $t_{n-1}$ critical value is often a useful approximation, but it is not design-exact for every finite population. Exact Student theory requires iid normal data. Strong skewness, heavy tails, rare binary outcomes, clustering, or a few dominant values can make either approximation poor.

### Interpretation in repeated finite-population sampling

Imagine repeatedly selecting independent SRSWOR samples of the same size from the same fixed population. Each sample produces its own interval. Approximate 95% coverage means that about 95% of these random intervals contain the same fixed $\overline{Y}$.

## Margin of error

The half-width of a symmetric confidence interval is called its margin of error.

<div class="definition" markdown="1">

**Definition 4.2** (Absolute margin of error). An absolute margin requirement is

$$
\mathbb{P}_p\bigl(|\widehat\theta-\theta|\le e\bigr)\approx1-\alpha,
$$

where $e>0$ is measured in the same units as $\theta$.

</div>

<div class="definition" markdown="1">

**Definition 4.3** (Relative margin of error). For nonzero $\theta$, a relative requirement is

$$
\mathbb{P}_p\left(
\left|\frac{\widehat\theta-\theta}{\theta}\right|
\le r
\right)\approx1-\alpha,
$$

where $r>0$ is dimensionless.

</div>

With a normal approximation, the planning equation is generally

$$
\text{margin of error}
=z_{\alpha/2}\times\text{planned standard error}.
$$

Sample-size choice is therefore an inversion of a variance formula.

## Why “a fixed percentage of the population” is the wrong starting rule

Precision depends on variability, confidence level, estimator, and design. It does not depend only on $n/N$. For estimating a mean under SRSWR,

$$
\mathop{\mathrm{SE}}(\overline{y})=\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt n},
$$

which contains no explicit $N$. A sample of 1,000 can be highly precise for a population of one million if variability is moderate and the design is sound; 10% of a population can be inadequate if the variable is highly variable or the design is clustered and biased.

The finite population fraction matters through the FPC when $n/N$ is not negligible. It is a correction to a precision calculation, not a substitute for one.

## Determining $n$ for a mean under SRSWR

Suppose a 100$(1-\alpha)$% interval with absolute half-width at most $e$ is required. Under SRSWR,

$$
e=z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt n}.
$$

Squaring and solving,

$$
\boxed{
n_{\mathrm{WR}}
=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\sigma_Y^2}{e^2}
}.
$$

Because sample size must be an integer and under-rounding violates the target, use

$$
n=\left\lceil n_{\mathrm{WR}}\right\rceil.
$$

**Question.**

The formula contains the unknown population variance. How can it be used before the survey?

**Answer.**

Use a defensible planning value obtained from a recent comparable survey, administrative data, a pilot sample, a scientifically justified upper bound, or a sensitivity analysis over several plausible standard deviations. If no information exists, conduct a pilot and then revise the main-sample calculation without pretending the pilot estimate is exact.

## Determining $n$ for a mean under SRSWOR

Under SRSWOR,

$$
\operatorname{Var}_{p}(\overline{y})=\left(1-\frac nN\right)\frac{S_Y^2}{n}.
$$

Set the normal half-width equal to $e$:

$$
e=z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\left(1-\frac nN\right)\frac{S_Y^2}{n}}.
$$

Square:

$$
e^2
=z_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2\left(\frac1n-\frac1N\right).
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac1n
=\frac{e^2}{z_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2}+\frac1N,
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
n_{\mathrm{WOR}}
=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2}
{e^2+z_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2/N}
=\frac{Nz_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2}
{Ne^2+z_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2}
}.
$$

Round upward.

Let

$$
n_0=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2S_Y^2}{e^2}
$$

be the with-replacement planning size using $S_Y^2$. Then

$$
\boxed{
n_{\mathrm{WOR}}=\frac{n_0}{1+n_0/N}}.
$$

If one instead begins with the denominator-$N$ variance $\sigma_Y^2$, the exact algebra gives

$$
n_{\mathrm{WOR}}
=\frac{Nz_{\alpha/2}^2\sigma_Y^2}
{(N-1)e^2+z_{\alpha/2}^2\sigma_Y^2}.
$$

Since $S_Y^2\approx\sigma_Y^2$ for large $N$, the slide’s relation

$$
n_{\mathrm{WOR}}
\approx\frac{n_{\mathrm{WR}}}{1+n_{\mathrm{WR}}/N}
$$

is the usual finite-population approximation.

## Relative precision

Suppose

$$
\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\overline{y}-\overline{Y}}{\overline{Y}}\right|\le r\right)
\approx1-\alpha.
$$

Under SRSWR, the planning equation is

$$
r\,|\overline{Y}|
=z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt n},
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
n
=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{r^2}
\left(\frac{\sigma_Y}{\overline{Y}}\right)^2
=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\,\mathop{\mathrm{CV}}_Y^2}{r^2}
},
$$

where $\mathop{\mathrm{CV}}_Y=\sigma_Y/\lvert\overline{Y}\rvert$ is the population coefficient of variation. Relative precision is therefore planned from a prior CV rather than from a prior variance alone.

## Sample size for a population proportion

For binary $Y$, $\sigma_Y^2=P(1-P)$. Under SRSWR,

$$
\boxed{
n_{\mathrm{WR}}
=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2P(1-P)}{e^2}
}.
$$

Under SRSWOR, using the exact denominator-$N$ form,

$$
\boxed{
n_{\mathrm{WOR}}
=\frac{Nz_{\alpha/2}^2P(1-P)}
{(N-1)e^2+z_{\alpha/2}^2P(1-P)}
}.
$$

The unknown factor $P(1-P)$ is maximized at $P=1/2$:

$$
P(1-P)=\frac14-\left(P-\frac12\right)^2\le\frac14.
$$

A conservative with-replacement size is therefore

$$
\boxed{
n_{\mathrm{WR,worst}}
=\frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{4e^2}
}.
$$

This guarantees the nominal normal-approximation half-width for every possible $P$ under the planning model, before considering nonresponse or design effects.

> **Caution.**
>
> The Wald interval $\widehat{P}\pm z\sqrt{\widehat{P}(1-\widehat{P})/n}$ can perform poorly when $n$ is small or $P$ is near 0 or 1. Wilson or score-based intervals are often preferable for binomial-style inference. In finite-population design-based work, the interval should also respect the FPC and actual design.

## Worked example: worst-case proportion

Suppose a 95% margin of error $e=0.05$ is required and no prior estimate of $P$ exists.

Under SRSWR,

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_0
&=\frac{1.96^2(0.5)(0.5)}{0.05^2}\\
&=384.16.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus take $n=385$.

If the finite population has $N=2000$ and SRSWOR will be used,

$$
\begin{aligned}
n
&=\frac{2000(1.96)^2(0.25)}
{1999(0.05)^2+(1.96)^2(0.25)}\\
&\approx322.40.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus take $n=323$ before nonresponse adjustment.

## Worked example: finite-population mean

Suppose $N=5000$, a prior study suggests $S_Y\approx400$, and a 95% half-width $e=50$ is required.

Ignoring the FPC,

$$
n_0
=\left(\frac{1.96\times400}{50}\right)^2
=245.8624,
$$

so the with-replacement requirement is 246.

With SRSWOR,

$$
n
=\frac{5000\times245.8624}{5000+245.8624}
\approx234.34,
$$

so take 235. The FPC saves 11 units relative to 246.

## Worked interpretation of the paying-guest example

The slide considers $n=15$ and an interval

$$
\text{Rs. }6000\pm\text{Rs. }1265
=[\text{Rs. }4735,\text{Rs. }7265].
$$

This interval is a consequence of the chosen sample and its variability; it does not show that $n=15$ was adequate for a policy purpose.

Suppose the intended half-width had instead been fixed at Rs. 300. If confidence level, design, and variance remain approximately unchanged, margin of error scales as $1/\sqrt n$. Thus

$$
\frac{1265}{300}
\approx\sqrt{\frac{n_{\mathrm{new}}}{15}},
$$

so

$$
n_{\mathrm{new}}
\approx15\left(\frac{1265}{300}\right)^2
\approx266.70.
$$

At least 267 observations would be required under this simple scaling approximation. A final calculation should use a planning variance and the actual finite-population or complex-design formula.

**Question.**

What happens if no margin of error is specified?

**Answer.**

There is no objective precision criterion against which to judge the chosen $n$. One can compute a confidence interval after collecting data, but its width may be unusably large. Sample-size planning should begin with a substantive precision target, not with an arbitrary sample count.

## Birth-weight illustration

The slide mentions a birth-weight margin such as $e=0.9$ kg. Whether this is “small” is a scientific and policy judgement; the slide does not provide a variance or confidence level, so no unique $n$ follows.

For illustration only, suppose prior data suggest $\sigma=0.60$ kg and the desired 95% half-width is $e=0.10$ kg. Then

$$
n
=\left(\frac{1.96\times0.60}{0.10}\right)^2
=138.2976,
$$

so take 139 births under SRSWR. If the desired half-width were $0.9$ kg instead, the mathematical requirement would be far smaller, illustrating why the substantive choice of $e$ dominates the calculation.

## Practical adjustments beyond the basic SRS formula

The basic formula assumes perfect response, the stated SRS design, and no nonsampling error. Real surveys often require adjustments.

### Anticipated nonresponse

If the required number of completed responses is $n$ and the anticipated response rate is $q$, invite approximately

$$
n_{\mathrm{invite}}=\left\lceil \frac nq\right\rceil.
$$

For example, 323 completed responses with an anticipated 80% response rate require

$$
\left\lceil 323/0.8\right\rceil=404
$$

sampled contacts. This arithmetic does not remove nonresponse bias; it only protects the achieved count.

### Design effect

For a complex design, define

$$
\operatorname{DEFF}
=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta\mid\text{actual design})}
{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta\mid\text{SRS of same nominal size})}.
$$

A first planning adjustment is

$$
n_{\mathrm{complex}}\approx n_{\mathrm{SRS}}\times\operatorname{DEFF}.
$$

Clustering often gives DEFF$>1$; effective stratification can give DEFF$<1$.

### Multiple outcomes and domains

If several variables are important, compute the requirement for each and use the largest, then check whether subgroup or domain estimates have enough cases. A sample adequate for a statewide mean may be inadequate for district-level estimates.

### Costs and feasibility

If the required $n$ is unaffordable, the legitimate options are to relax precision, lower confidence, improve the design, use stronger auxiliary information, reduce the number of reporting domains, or reconsider whether the survey is worthwhile. Quietly collecting a much smaller sample does not preserve the original guarantee.

> **Lecture summary.**
>
> - A confidence interval is a random interval with repeated-sampling coverage.
> - Under SRSWOR, the standard approximate interval is $\overline{y}\pm z\sqrt{1-f}\,s/\sqrt n$.
> - Sample size is obtained by setting the desired margin equal to $z$ times a planned standard error and solving for $n$.
> - For SRSWOR, $n=n_0/(1+n_0/N)$ when $n_0=z^2S_Y^2/e^2$.
> - For an unknown proportion, $P=1/2$ gives the conservative worst case.
> - Variance information, nonresponse, design effect, and domain requirements must be incorporated into real planning.

## Answers to questions posed in the slides

### 30. Is the distinct-units estimator better than the usual SRSWR mean?

Yes in the variance sense: it equals $E_p(\overline{y}\mid D)$ and is therefore a Rao–Blackwell improvement. It is still generally less efficient than directly drawing $n$ distinct units by SRSWOR.

### 31. How should the sample size $n$ be chosen?

Specify the estimand, confidence level, tolerable absolute or relative error, design, anticipated variability, and practical constraints. Solve the resulting margin-of-error equation, round upward, and adjust for nonresponse and complex design.

### 32. What is the “right” $n$?

There is no universal right number. It is the smallest feasible size that achieves the stated inferential objective under the planned design and credible variance assumptions, while allowing for field realities.

### 33. How much precision is needed, and how much error is tolerable?

These are substantive decisions, not purely statistical ones. Policy stakes, clinical relevance, reporting domains, and cost determine the acceptable margin $e$ or relative margin $r$.

### 34. What if no margin of error is specified?

An arbitrary $n$ cannot be judged adequate before data collection. The resulting interval may be too wide. Precision should be specified first and $n$ derived from it.
